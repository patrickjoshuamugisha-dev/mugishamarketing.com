AI in Danish businesses: figures and sources 2026

Last updated: 21 November 2026

In 2026, 59% of Danish companies with ten or more employees used AI, up from 15% in 2023 (Statistics Denmark). In 2025 Denmark had the highest share in the EU: 42.03% against 19.95% across the EU (Eurostat). The AI Act applies from 2 August 2026, with later dates for high-risk systems (European Commission).

How many Danish companies use AI?

Statistics Denmark asks a sample of private, non-financial companies with ten or more employees every year whether they use at least one AI technology. The reference date is January. The share rose from 15% in 2023 to 28% in 2024, 42% in 2025 and 59% in 2026.

All (10 or more employees)15%28%42%59%
10-49 employees12%24%37%55%
50-99 employees19%38%56%69%
100-249 employees29%46%62%79%
250 employees and over52%63%74%88%

Source: Statistics Denmark, table ITAV28, share of enterprises using artificial intelligence, by size.

The gap between small and large companies has narrowed in percentage points: in 2023, companies with 10-49 employees were 40 points below those with 250 or more; in 2026 the gap is 33.

By sector, the share in 2026 was highest in IT and media (89%) and in business services and repair of consumer goods (68%), and lowest in construction (43%). Industry and utilities stood at 56%, and trade, transport, accommodation and restaurants at 55%.

Statistics Denmark also counts which kinds of AI technology companies use. Text dominates. The share that automates workflows with AI is smaller but rose from 15% to 25% in a year.

On the benefit, the Danish Agency for Digital Government writes that “seven in ten of those who use AI” find they are more efficient in their workflows, and that more than half see better products or services (report of 4 April 2025). Dansk Erhverv writes that 73% of the companies in the survey report more efficient workflows and 27% see a direct effect on revenue and earnings (analysis of February 2026). Dansk Erhverv’s figures cover its members, not all companies.

What holds companies back?

Eurostat asks the companies that have considered AI why they do not use it. In Denmark, 28.04% of the companies that do not use AI had considered it, against 14.21% in the EU. Among Danish companies that had considered it, lack of knowledge is the most common reason:

Reason for not using AI, 2025DenmarkEU
A lack of relevant expertise82.06%70.31%
A lack of clarity about the legal consequences47.70%53.61%
Concerns about violating data protection and privacy45.53%52.72%
The costs seem too high17.99%38.37%
AI is not useful for the enterprise16.05%17.79%

Source: Eurostat, the dataset (isoc_eb_ai). The figures are shares of the enterprises that had considered using AI. The percentages do not add up to 100, so companies evidently could give more than one reason.

The Danish Agency for Digital Government reaches the same picture: companies that do not use AI explain it “most often by a lack of relevant knowledge and experience and by considerations about compliance with data protection and privacy rules, rather than technical and economic limits” (page on the report; our translation). In Dansk Erhverv’s member survey the biggest barriers are lack of staff skills (36%), lack of insight and knowledge (30%) and lack of time (30%).

Dansk Industri (DI) writes in its analysis of 16 September 2026 that almost 80%% of the companies it asked use AI “in one form or another”, but that for 42%% the use is ad hoc, while 24%% use AI systematically in selected departments and 11%% in almost all departments. DI notes itself that its figures cannot be compared directly with Eurostat’s, because AI use is defined differently. The analysis does not say how many were asked.

Denmark compared with the EU

Eurostat writes that the share of enterprises using AI in 2025 ranged between 5.21% and 42.03% across EU countries. The highest share was in Denmark (42.03%), followed by Finland (37.82%) and Sweden (35.04%), the lowest in Romania (5.21%). The largest rise from 2024 to 2025 was in Denmark: 14.45 percentage points.

In relative terms the lead is largest among the small: with 10-49 employees, 37.45% of Danish companies used AI in 2025 against 17.00% in the EU, more than double. In the Denmark 2025 country report the European Commission writes that “a notable gap exists between large and small companies in adopting cutting-edge digital technologies”.

Eurostat plans its next update of the article for 15 December 2026. Statistics Denmark’s 2026 figure (59%) therefore has no EU figure to be compared with yet.

The rules: AI Act dates

The European Commission writes that the AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and became applicable on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions. The dates as the Commission gives them:

  • 2 February 2025: prohibited AI practices and AI literacy obligations.
  • 2 August 2025: governance rules and obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models.
  • 2 August 2026: the Act is applicable, with exceptions.
  • December 2026: the ninth prohibition, on AI systems that generate non-consensual sexually explicit and intimate content or child sexual abuse material, introduced as part of the AI Omnibus (read on the Commission’s page on 21 November 2026).
  • 2 December 2027: rules for high-risk use cases in certain sensitive areas, including biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control (Annex III).
  • 2 August 2028: rules for high-risk AI systems embedded in regulated products, such as lifts or toys (Annex I).

The dates for the high-risk rules have been extended. For Annex III the Commission writes that this follows the political agreement on the simplification proposal, the “AI Omnibus”. According to the Commission the proposal was adopted on 19 November 2025, a political agreement was reached on 7 May 2026, and it entered into force on 27 July 2026. We do not give legal advice. Ask your adviser what the dates mean for your use.

What this means for a company with 10 to 500 staff

This section is our reading of the figures, not something the sources say.

For most companies the question is no longer whether they use AI but how deeply. When 55% of companies with 10-49 employees and 69-79% of those with 50-249 already use AI in 2026, use by itself is not a difference. DI’s figures suggest that the largest group uses it ad hoc.

The figures show mostly text use: 45% use AI to analyse text and 44% to write it, against 25% to automate workflows. We read that as many companies having tools for writing and reading, while fewer have connected AI to the systems the work runs through. That is a conclusion we draw, not something the statistics measure directly.

Among Danish companies that had considered AI, a lack of knowledge is the most common reason for not using it (82.06%), and cost is named far less often (17.99%). We take that as a sign that the first step is often to pick one concrete task and settle who owns it before choosing a tool. See also how an AI implementation usually runs.

Frequently asked questions

How many Danish companies use AI?

According to Statistics Denmark, 59% of private companies with ten or more employees used AI in 2026, against 42% in 2025, 28% in 2024 and 15% in 2023. The figure covers at least one AI technology and is measured in January each year.

How does Denmark compare with the rest of the EU?

Denmark is first. Eurostat found in 2025 that 42.03% of Danish companies with ten or more employees used AI, against 19.95% in the EU as a whole. Finland was second with 37.82% and Sweden third with 35.04%.

Do smaller companies use AI?

Yes, and the share is rising fast. At Statistics Denmark, 55% of companies with 10-49 employees used AI in 2026, against 12% in 2023. For companies with 50-99 employees the figure was 69%, and for 100-249 employees 79%.

What holds companies back from using AI?

At Eurostat, 82.06% of Danish companies that had considered AI give a lack of relevant expertise as a reason for not using it. 47.70% name unclear legal consequences and 45.53% data protection. Cost is named by 17.99%.

When does the AI Act apply?

The European Commission writes that it entered into force on 1 August 2024 and became applicable on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions. The bans and the AI literacy obligations apply from 2 February 2025, and the rules for high-risk systems from 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028.

What to know before you quote

  • Statistics Denmark, Eurostat, Dansk Erhverv and DI measure AI use differently and cannot be added together. Dansk Erhverv’s figures cover its members, and the pages we read do not say how they were selected.
  • Statistics Denmark’s footnote to ITAV28 (our translation): “The reference date is January of the year in question. Values for 2023-2025 have been recalculated back in time using the new DB25 sector codes. The values can therefore differ from previously published values.” The Danish Agency for Digital Government gave 75% for companies with 250 or more employees in 2025 in September 2025, while the table today shows 74%. We have not found a stated reason; the footnote is one possible explanation.
  • Eurostat says its data come from surveys by national statistical authorities in the first months of the year, and Statistics Denmark’s reference date is January. Both give 42% for Denmark in 2025.
  • Eurostat’s reasons for not using AI are shares of the companies that had considered AI. They are not shares of all companies.
